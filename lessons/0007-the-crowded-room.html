The Crowded Room

Who sets the board, and why the party gets less clever as it fills up.

The one idea: User and system tokens are the fixed board position you set by hand; the model only plays from there — which is why they're enormous leverage. And a fixed attention budget spread across a crowded room is why long contexts dilute, neglect the middle, and fall into loops.

An applied lesson, straight for your work building with LLMs and agents — built entirely from mechanics you already have: prefill vs decode, the finite dance card, the frozen past, and induction heads.

Movement I · Who sets the board
🎬 Setting the Board user/system tokens are prefilled, not generated User and system tokens are read in (prefill) — run through the layers to build their representations — but the model doesn't sample at those positions. Sampling begins only at the assistant's turn. So the prompt is the board position you set by hand; the model then plays every move from there. You place the stones; it responds.
🎭 One Uniform, Many Costumes the role distinction is a learned convention Mechanically there is no difference in how user vs assistant tokens are processed — same layers, same attention. The distinction is a costume: special role-marker tokens (the chat template) plus fine-tuning taught the model "text after the user marker is instruction; after the assistant marker is my own voice to continue." A convention painted onto a uniform machine.
Movement II · Why the prompt is such high leverage

Three reasons, all mechanical:

  • Upstream of everything. By the frozen-past/causal structure, every generated token attends back to the prompt. The system prompt, sitting at the very start, is in the left-context of literally every token — maximal reach.
  • Noise-free anchors. The prompt isn't sampled — it's exact. Generated tokens carry sampling noise; your tokens don't.
  • Few-shot examples get copied. Induction heads (Lesson 6) copy patterns from the prompt directly — so demonstrations are leverage on top of leverage.
🎲 Deterministic Dynamics, One Die deterministic pass + sampling; attractor basins The forward pass is deterministic; the only randomness is the sampling die at the readout. So runs diverge in exact words but usually re-converge in meaning — the model's probability mass pulls trajectories back into the same attractor basin. Caveat: at a genuine fork or high temperature, an early divergence can cascade into a wholly different response. Usually convergent, occasionally chaotic.
The practical rule: you set the board; the model plays. Editing the prompt propagates to the entire downstream trajectory; editing a late generated token changes little. That asymmetry is why prompt and system-message design is the highest-leverage knob you hold.
Movement III · Why the crowded room gets less clever

As context fills, quality tends to decline (a rough rule of thumb, not a law — it varies by model and task, and long-context models soften it). It's not one cause but four, and you can read each off the mechanics:

👥 The Finite Heart attention dilution Every steward's dance card still sums to one. With a handful of guests, the relevant few get deep pours. With hundreds of thousands, that same fixed heart spreads thin — the signal you care about competes with a throng of distractors, and softmax dilutes it. A fixed attention budget, ever more claimants.
🕳️ Lost in the Middle positional attention bias Models empirically privilege the start (which set the tone) and the most recent guests (freshest), and under-attend the vast middle. Bury a key fact at the midpoint of a long context and it whispers from the back of the hall.
📏 A Room Bigger Than Rehearsal long context is out-of-distribution Training saw mostly shorter sequences. Pack in far more guests than the party ever rehearsed with and the Host's seating sense (positional encoding) degrades at the far edges — the machinery is simply less practised out there.
🔁 The Echo That Feeds Itself induction-head looping The elegant failure. The very stewards that make the model clever at following patterns — induction heads, which find the earlier occurrence and copy what followed — will, once the recent output turns repetitive, see the repetition and copy it again, reinforcing the loop. And as context fills with the model's own tokens, it's increasingly conditioned on itself — an echo chamber drifting from the original instruction.
Upshots you can act on today:
  • Put crucial content early or late, never buried in the middle of a long context.
  • Keep contexts lean — every irrelevant token is a distractor stealing from a fixed attention budget.
  • Beware feeding the model long stretches of its own repetitive output; it feeds the echo.
  • Invest in the system/user message — it's upstream of, and attended by, everything.

The gears beneath this lesson

  • Setting the Board → user/system tokens are prefilled, not sampled; generation starts at the assistant turn.
  • One Uniform, Many Costumes → the role distinction is chat-template tokens + fine-tuning, over a uniform mechanism.
  • High leverage → the prompt is upstream (maximal attention reach), noise-free, and copied by induction heads.
  • Deterministic Dynamics, One Die → deterministic pass + sampling → divergent words, usually convergent meaning (occasionally chaotic).
  • The four crowd effects → attention dilution, lost-in-the-middle, out-of- distribution length, and self-reinforcing induction-head loops.
