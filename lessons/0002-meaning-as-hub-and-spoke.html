Meaning as Hub and Spoke

Where a concept physically lives — and why your "tokens are a barrier to meaning" hunch turns out to be a precise, testable claim about missing hardware.

First, recall (from Lesson 1)

Before reading on: in one sentence, what does a top-down goal signal do in biased competition? If it doesn't come back instantly, reopen Lesson 1 for ten seconds — spacing the recall is what moves it from fluency to storage.

You've already reasoned about the LLM side of meaning: a word's meaning is its position in a vector space learned from which tokens co-occur with which. Meaning is purely relational — a token defined by other tokens. Your instinct was that this is a barrier: language pointing only at more language, never at the world. This lesson gives that instinct a mechanism, by showing what the brain has that the embedding space doesn't.

The problem: one concept, many modalities

Think of the concept apple. It has a look (red, round), a feel, a taste, a heft in the hand, the action of biting, the word "apple," the word "manzana." These live in different brain regions — vision, touch, motor, language. So what makes them one concept rather than seven disconnected fragments? What lets the smell alone summon the name, and the name summon the look?

The answer: spokes feed a hub

The dominant account is the hub-and-spoke model 1. Meaning is built from two parts:

The hub's job is to capture deep similarity that no single spoke can see. A robin and a sparrow share almost no specific pixels, sounds, or words — but they are alike as concepts. That likeness only exists once features from every modality are fused into one representation. The hub is where "meaning" as distinct from "features" is built 1.

How we know it's real

Two lines of evidence, one natural and one experimental:

  • Semantic dementia. When atrophy is focused on the anterior temporal lobe, concepts degrade across every modality at once — the patient can't name a picture, recognise the object, match it to a sound, or understand the word. Damage the hub and the whole concept blurs, no matter how you probe it 2.
  • rTMS in healthy brains. Temporarily disrupting the ATL with magnetic stimulation slows semantic judgements in neurologically normal people — briefly mimicking the disorder, which shows the hub is doing this job in every brain 3.

Damage a spoke instead, and you get a modality-specific hole — a problem with, say, colours or actions, while the rest of the concept survives. Hub versus spoke damage is the whole model's fingerprint.

Now: what the LLM is missing

Word embeddings are, in effect, a hub-like relational space with no spokes. They fuse statistics across tokens into positions that capture conceptual similarity — genuinely hub-like. But every dimension is grounded only in more tokens. There is no vision spoke, no action spoke, nothing pointing at the world. This is the classic symbol grounding problem 4: symbols defined entirely by other symbols.

Parallel that holds

The embedding space and the ATL hub do the same kind of work: distilling many scattered features into a modality-invariant point that captures deep similarity. The LLM really does build a hub. Your "tokens as barrier" intuition is not that the hub is fake.

Where it breaks — the precise version of your hunch

The barrier isn't the hub; it's the missing spokes. A human concept is a hub fed by sensory-motor experience of the world. An embedding is a hub fed only by text describing the world. Mechanically, "language is a barrier to meaning for an LLM" = the model has the integrator but not the grounding it's supposed to integrate.
